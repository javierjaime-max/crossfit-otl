# Who We Are — coach clips

Seven short clips. Record them in one sitting, phone on a tripod at the whiteboard, or deliver them live at a coach meeting and send the recording after. Each one is two to four minutes spoken. Speaker tags mark who says what. Everything here is from the two recordings, tightened for the camera.

Read these out loud before recording. Change any line that doesn't sound like you.

---

## Clip 0 — Why we're doing this (Javier, ~2 min)

Hey coaches. Deanie and I sat down this week and made a list of the things we need everybody focused on, and we wanted to send it to you in a few short clips instead of a long meeting.

First, the good news. You guys follow the class plan. We start on time and we finish on time, and that's a common experience for every member no matter who's coaching. We've been doing it that way since day one, and it's one of the reasons people appreciate us.

What we're seeing now is the little things. Somebody finishes a workout early and goes and sits at the front table on their phone. Somebody starts putting their weights away while three people are still working. People are in the back putting their shoes on while the coach is trying to do the whiteboard. None of it is a big deal by itself. The problem is that people follow what they see. If somebody with 500 check-ins on the Board does it, the new person thinks that's just how it works here. That's what we're calling drift, and it's contagious.

We sell coaching. We don't sell access to a gym, and we don't sell somebody standing there running a clock. In a coached environment people are watched. They get corrected, they get encouraged, they get challenged, and their names get spoken. That's what people are paying for.

One more thing before the list, though, and it's important. None of this is about being strict. We're not going to punish anybody or single anybody out. If somebody has to walk in five minutes late every single day for a season because there's no way to get across town, we want them here. If somebody has to leave early, they're still welcome. The expectation of being here from minute zero to minute sixty is not about control. It's about impact. It's us giving people the most for what they're investing in this place.

So the list is six things. One clip each. Here we go.

## Clip 1 — For-time workouts: nobody leaves the floor (Javier, ~3 min)

The first one is for-time workouts.

Here's what happens. We've got people who scale so far down that they finish at the very low end of the stimulus, sometimes well under it. Other people are at the top end of the stimulus. So you end up with athletes sitting there for minutes waiting for the workout to end, and they drift. They go to their phones. They sit on the benches under the rig. They go to the front table. And the worst version is when three or four of them do it together and walk off the floor as a group. Now the class is split in two, with half the people still working.

I'd put that right next to putting your weights away before everybody's done. We've always said not to do that, and only a few people still do it. Some of it is a language thing and some of it is just that they're new. Leaving the floor early and getting on your phone is not far behind it on the list of things we don't do here.

So this is what I need from you on every for-time piece.

Say it at the Briefing, before 3-2-1. Tell them the stimulus. Tell them people are going to finish at different times. Tell them that when they finish, they stay on the floor. Not standing over anybody. Some people don't want a crowd in their ear while they're working. Just present, so nobody finishes to an empty room. And tell them not to put their weights away until you say it's time.

Say why, though. Don't just say the rule. The reason we do this in a room instead of in a garage by ourselves is that nobody finishes alone. And here's the other piece. If somebody finishes way early, that's information. Plant the question for them: what could you have added today? That's the same conversation as the last clip, and it starts here.

And when the last person finishes, your voice is the next thing everybody hears. Deanie's going to talk about that in the next couple of clips.

## Clip 2 — The Briefing: the hour starts here (Javier + Deanie, ~4 min)

**[Javier]** The second one is the whiteboard. The Briefing.

What I see is the coach starts the Briefing, two or three people walk up, and the rest are in the back putting on their shoes or talking to each other. A lot of them are long-time members who've read the workout on their phone already and feel like they've heard it before.

Their brain might not be all the way there, but their body needs to be present. And the Briefing is not a four-minute reading of a workout they already read. It's the stimulus. It's the scaling. It's safety and logistics. It's all the stuff in the coach's notes that the member can't see from their phone. If what we say at the whiteboard isn't worth walking up for, that's on us. Know your notes.

Me personally, I don't start until everybody's up there. Today there were people talking behind the rig while I was trying to start, and I just stopped and said, hey, everybody come forward. If you do that every single time, they learn that's how class starts. If you do it some days and not others, they drift. And it's not one person drifting. If somebody sees somebody else in the back tying their shoes, they think that's fine. If they see somebody walk in during the warm-up, they think that's fine too.

**[Deanie]** The reason it matters is that the Briefing is the start of your leadership for the hour. If you don't have the group at the very beginning, you won't have them for the rest of it. The most compliance you will ever get is in the first few seconds. If you don't have it then, it just goes downhill from there.

And there's a way to do it without being bitchy about it. Nobody's going to walk over because you stood there with a face on and said "Deanie, come to the front." I'm probably not coming. Anybody with a rebel streak either doesn't come back to your class or walks over mad.

So read the room. At five o'clock there's people finishing the four, people there for Development Time, people changing in the bathroom. You don't know who's yours yet. So it's, "Hey, I'm coaching five PM. Raise your hand if you're a five PM-er. Okay, everybody who raised their hand, one minute, meet me at the whiteboard. We're jump roping today, grab a rope on your way over." Calm, friendly, and firm. Kind of like getting kindergartners to line up. That is part of coaching. That is the job.

**[Javier]** So the practical version. The class starts at the top of the hour, but your hour starts a few minutes before that. If you're in the middle of a conversation in the back of the gym at 4:58 and then you yell for everybody at 5:00, you've already lost it. Be in the room. Let them know class is starting in a couple minutes. And when the clock hits, look across the room, and if somebody's not with you, wait for them. Call them over. If it costs you twenty seconds the first few weeks, spend the twenty seconds. We're training people how class starts here, and that only works if all of us do it the same way.

That's the last thing. It has to be every coach. We can't have a member figure out that this coach makes you come to the whiteboard and that coach doesn't, so I'll go to that one. That can't be the experience.

## Clip 3 — Sixty minutes on the floor (Deanie + Javier, ~3 min)

**[Deanie]** The third one is you, during the workout.

If you're behind the coach's table or on your phone during the WOD, you can't expect to have command of the room when it's over. You coach the entire twenty-minute AMRAP. You coach the entire five-to-seven-minute for-time. You're on the floor, in the line of sight of every one of them. The second you leave the floor, it's like the teacher walking out of the classroom. You lose it.

So when people start putting equipment away while somebody's still working, that's on you. That's not on them. That is a coaching issue, not an athlete issue. You stepped off your podium and you handed the floor to the civilians. It is up to you to make sure they know you are the coach for the whole hour, and you're leading what happens every single minute of it.

**[Javier]** We put that table up front for the stuff we use at the start and end of class. We did not put it there to be a place to stand during the workout. Deanie's word for it was a crutch.

There's an old idea from corporate offices called managing by walking around. The manager doesn't have to say anything. He just leaves his office and walks the floor, and suddenly everybody's a little more productive and a little more aware of what they're doing. Same thing here. Walk. Put your eyes on one athlete at a time and look for one thing to fix or one thing to compliment. You don't have to fix all twenty things. There's a whole set of coaching principles for that and it's not this clip. The point is that's the job. If you start the clock and check out until it ends, or you let yourself get pulled into a conversation, or you hang out at the table, you've missed the point of a coached environment.

**[Deanie]** And I'll just say it. If you're not spent after you coach, you didn't do it right. Coaching back-to-back classes should wear you out. Mentally, physically, emotionally. If it doesn't, it wasn't done all the way.

## Clip 4 — FORD: know your people (Deanie + Javier, ~2 min)

**[Javier]** Number four is short. FORD. Family, occupation, recreation, dreams. If you know those four things about your people, they feel seen, and people keep coming back to places where somebody knows their name and knows what's going on in their life. Most of you do this really well already. This is just naming it.

**[Deanie]** FORD is also how you tell the difference between somebody drifting and somebody having a rough day.

Somebody had a combative teenager last night. Somebody's going through a divorce. Somebody's spouse has been traveling all week and they've had the kids by themselves. It's Friday and they've been here four straight days and everything hurts. They show up, they scale it way down, and they just move their body. That is fine. That's not something to comment on. That's "I know where you are. I see you. I'm glad you're here." Sometimes just walking through the door was the hard part.

That's not the same as somebody who stays on the twelve-inch box every single class when you know they can do twenty. One is a day. The other is a pattern. You only know which one it is if you know the person.

**[Javier]** And that's the point. If we just let people in the door to work out on their own, that'd be a different kind of gym. We know our people. Keep doing it.

## Clip 5 — Everybody helps everybody (Javier + Deanie, ~3 min)

**[Javier]** Number five is putting equipment away, and this one we've had in our heads as a family for years and I don't think we've ever said it out loud to you or to the members. So here it is.

When we finish a workout, you say it's time to put the weights away. Most people wait for that, and that's great. But here's what's happening after that. Somebody who's newer has a bar, a couple of change plates, and some clips. They're done in ten seconds. Somebody else went RX. They've got six heavy plates on the bar, the bar is at the far end of the room from the weight stack, and they're still on the floor because they were at the very edge of the stimulus. Then the cool-down starts before they've even stood up, so everybody goes and joins you and leaves them to it.

That's not who we are. We don't move on to the next part until everybody's there. We don't leave the general warm-up until everybody's done with it. Same thing here. Everybody helps everybody until everything is put away, and then we start the cool-down.

So say it out loud. Every class. "Everybody help everybody put your weights away. We start the cool-down when all the equipment is up." The order matters. You say it's time, they put their weights away, and then they look for ways to help clear the floor. Somebody else's plates, the clips, the chalk. They keep working until everybody's done. If everybody does it, everybody finishes faster. It's not about fair. It's just a group of people that work together.

**[Deanie]** And the coach does it too. As soon as the workout's over, your voice is the first thing they hear. "Time. Everybody clean up. Everybody help everybody until it's away. When we're done, meet me here." Then you go pick up plates yourself, and you grab a different person each class to help you. That's how they learn it.

## Clip 6 — A little more than last time (Javier + Deanie, ~3 min)

**[Javier]** The last one is stimulus, and it ties back to the first clip.

If somebody finishes way under the stimulus every single time, that's drift too. It's over-scaling. We're not going to shame anybody for it. But if people are finishing so early that they've got five minutes to sit around, then we're also not doing our job in the specific warm-up. That's where you set the weight. That's where you find out what somebody can actually handle today.

So encourage people to move between the tracks. Not RX. Not RX plus. Sometimes it's just two and a half pound plates. Sometimes it's five pounds. Sometimes it's one more round. A little more than last time, when last time felt easy.

Here's why it matters, and I'll just say it plainly. Somebody who pays us long enough to get 500 check-ins is valuable. But when somebody in our gym gets their first pull-up, or runs their first mile, or gets their first muscle-up, or loses that fifty pounds, they become a walking billboard for our gym. They sing our praises, not because we asked them to, but because you as a coach gave them everything you had, and that's what made the transformation they came in here for.

We have some clients who literally just need to move. We also have some who have never been pushed or challenged in their life to realize their full potential. You have that opportunity. Not to force people, not to shame people, not to make sure every workout they're sprawled out on the floor. But to make sure you were there, so they can be challenged to achieve things they never thought they could. And when they do, either they sing your praises, or people notice they're not the same person they were before they walked into our gym. Those are the best referrals we could ever ask for.

Change is adaptation, and adaptation comes from challenge. That's true for us as coaches too. We forget it sometimes.

**[Deanie]** And go back to the FORD clip. Somebody having a rough week who scales it down once or twice, leave them alone. That's fine. It's the person who does it every single class, week after week, who can do more. That's the conversation. You know your people, so you know which one it is.

**[Javier]** That's the six. Nobody leaves the floor on a for-time. The hour starts at the whiteboard and you own it from a few minutes before. Sixty minutes on the floor. Know your people. Everybody helps everybody. A little more than last time.

We're going to put this in front of the members too, in the app and printed in the gym. But it goes to you first, because it only works if every coach does it the same way. Thanks, guys.
