# CrossFit OTL — Philosophy & Voice

## The Core Belief

CrossFit OTL was never built around competitive fitness. It was built around **life change**.

> "My story in CrossFit has never been about competitive CrossFit. I'm competitive with myself. The things that brought us to where we are have been about lifestyle change, life change, health restoration — and those things happened not only with me but with my wife, my daughter, my son, my mom, my sister-in-law. All people whose lives were affected because I found CrossFit 10–12 years ago."

The ripple started with Javier. It spread to Deanie. Then to Isabel and Nicholas. Then to the garage group. Then to the affiliate. That's the model — and it's the story OTL tells.

---

## What OTL Values (Unwritten, But Real)

- **Know your members.** Every coach has a photo on file. When someone walks in, call them by name — intentionally, every time.
- **Care about the why.** When someone comes in, understand why they're there. Don't customize the programming, but customize the message — what will actually benefit this person.
- **The lifestyle, not just the workout.** CrossFit is not a place you work out. It's a way of being. The daily feedback from your body about what you ate and how you slept — that feedback loop is the lifestyle.
- **Family is the core audience.** Not competitive athletes. Families. Adults with teens. Kids. Homeschool communities. The gym was literally built for a family to run and for families to attend.
- **Professionalism over friendship.** A slight separation between coaches and members is intentional. Alcohol blurs lines. Party culture creates instability. OTL stays out of that. Care for everyone — but stay on the outside of friend groups cycling in and out.

## On Programming Families — Why Kids and Teens Run With Adults, Year-Round

This is a deliberate scheduling decision, not a logistical one. Most CrossFit affiliates run kids classes after school as a separate program. OTL doesn't.

**Kids and Teens classes run simultaneously with adult classes.** When mom and dad are training, the kids and teens are training. Same room or adjacent space, same time block. The family arrives together, trains together, leaves together.

**Year-round, not by season.** Most families let athletics shape their kids' fitness — football season, soccer season, basketball season. When the season ends, so does the kid's fitness. OTL runs Kids and Teens classes year-round on the same calendar as adults so fitness becomes a constant, not a seasonal activity tied to school athletics.

### Why this design

We want families to build a habit set of health and fitness as a **lifestyle**, not as a sports season. The two produce very different adults.

- **Sports-season fitness** ends when the season ends. The kid associates training with the team, the coach, the schedule someone else set. When the team goes away, the training goes away. Most kids never re-engage as adults — they peaked at 17 and rode it down for the next 40 years.
- **Lifestyle fitness** is what the family does. The kid doesn't associate it with a season or a team — they associate it with mom, dad, the family default. When the school sports end, nothing changes at OTL. The lifestyle continues. The installation holds.

Let athletics shape ability. Let the lifestyle shape the body, the relationship to movement, and the long-term default. Those are different jobs. OTL is built to deliver the second one.

### Why this matters for OTL specifically

This is where the gym's deepest competitive moat lives. Other CrossFit affiliates can match programming, pricing, and even coaching quality. They can't match scheduling that's been deliberately designed around a generational transfer thesis — because most haven't thought about it that way.

It's also why "Family is the core audience" (in the values section above) is operational, not aspirational. The schedule, the coaching team (mostly family), and the year-round commitment are all expressions of the same belief: this is how a family installs the lifestyle into the next generation.

### How to talk about it

When parents ask "what's the difference between OTL and a regular CrossFit gym for my kid?" — this is the answer. Not "we have great kids coaches." The answer is: **"Your kid trains while you train. Year-round. So fitness becomes your family's default, not a season they finish and forget."**

## On Coaching

> "When you make it so simple for kids, then when you teach an adult — the more complicated you make it, the harder it is. Teaching kids is actually better training for coaching adults."

- Isabel coaching kids at 14 made her a better coach for adults.
- The L2 changed how Javier thinks about class structure — specifically the idea that a strength workout *can be* the whole hour. CAP programming builds this in.
- Class management: the rotation model (one person lifts at a time, coach sees every rep) vs. the split-rig model (half the class goes, then the other half) — used depending on the movement and the group's comfort level.
- The 5am, 6am, and 6pm class crowds are the Mid-Cities working family. They show up. They do the work.

## On Nutrition

The whole family has been through: Zone → Paleo → Intermittent Fasting → Keto → Carnivore.

Key moments:
- Deanie's GERD, skin issues, headaches all cleared within days of going Paleo. Surgery cancelled.
- This is what committed the family — not competition outcomes, but measurable health results.
- Isabel's senior thesis on Ancel Keys and the sugar industry — the entire food pyramid was built on corrupted science, political capture, and corporate greed.

OTL's nutrition philosophy: teach the why. Don't just tell people what to eat — explain the mechanism. Isabel and Nicholas grew up reading the same books, articles, and CrossFit Journal pieces Javier was reading. They know it because they lived it.

## On Business

- **Two-Brain framework.** Business is not intuition — it's systems. Two-Brain gave OTL the playbook, pricing, sales process, and mentoring it needed to go from garage to affiliate without amateur mistakes.
- **CAP programming.** Not writing your own programming doesn't make you a lesser affiliate. CAP has clear RX/Intermediate/Beginner tracks built in. New coaches learn from every class. Members from other gyms consistently comment on the class *flow*.
- **The no-sweat intro.** Every new member comes in for a conversation before they join. Not a trial class. A conversation. See if it's a fit — for them and for OTL.
- **Pricing is not the conversation.** If someone's comparing OTL to Planet Fitness, OTL can't help them. That's okay.

## OTL's Voice (How to Sound Like the Gym)

- Direct. Not preachy.
- Personal. Stories over statistics — then statistics to back the story.
- Family-first. Not "performance." Not "athletes." People.
- Honest about the hard part. CrossFit is not easy. That's the point.
- Never motivational-poster soft. "You've got this" is not an OTL phrase.
- Credentialed. Every coaching cue has a reason. The L3/CCFT path is the proof.

**Things OTL doesn't do:**
- Compete with Planet Fitness on price
- Promise quick results
- Party with members
- Blast homeschool identity as a marketing angle
- Pretend CrossFit is for everyone (it's for anyone willing — not everyone)

## The Attrition Thresholds — Why People Quit CrossFit (And When)

This is Javier's observed framework for where people drop out, and why. It's useful for the consultation process, content, and coaching — because each threshold maps to a specific fear or disappointment that OTL can address.

**Threshold 1: Never start.** Too afraid to walk through the door. Friends or family warned them off. A bad secondhand story convinced them. They never begin.

**Threshold 2: Walk in and walk out.** They come in once, see people doing burpees, pull-ups off a rig, barbells moving in unfamiliar ways, and think: there's no way I can do this. I'm not fit enough. I'm not strong enough. They leave without giving it a chance.

**Threshold 3: Quit after one week.** They overcome the initial fear — maybe prodded by a family member. A great gym will have talked them through their reservations. They start. Then they experience soreness unlike anything they've felt before. Not because CrossFit is uniquely hard, but because every single class they'll work harder than they would alone, using muscle groups they've never used. They quit because they don't like being uncomfortable. The discomfort would have passed if they gave it time.

**Threshold 4: Quit after one month.** They survive the soreness. They try a few classes. Then their ego takes a hit — the stay-at-home moms, the grandmothers, are moving well and strongly at levels they haven't reached yet. They feel embarrassed. Or maybe they only showed up two or three days, the soreness never fully resolved, and they wanted quick results that 30 days of CrossFit didn't deliver. CrossFit is a long-term program with a long adaptation curve — but they wanted a short-term fix.

**Threshold 5: Quit after three months.** The initial rush — the urgency that made them walk in — dissipated. Their schedule got busy. They went from 10 classes in month one to five in month two to zero in month three. They slowly stopped without making a decision to stop.

**After three months, the odds flip.** If someone makes it past three months, the likelihood of making it to six months is high. The likelihood of making it to a year is good. Three months is the critical gate.

**Threshold 6: Quit between one and three years.** This is the injury window. CrossFit does challenge the body — and unless someone has been continuously active throughout their life, setbacks are essentially inevitable. But the same would be true in a conventional gym, a running program, triathlon training, or Spartan racing. The difference is that in CrossFit, people are sometimes more willing to attribute the injury to the sport. They take a couple of days off "to be safe," extend to a week, and quietly stop returning.

**The underlying pattern:** people quit when they hit something uncomfortable that they didn't anticipate. The job of the consultation — and of OTL's content — is to name each of those thresholds before the prospect reaches them, so they aren't surprised.

## On Fear of CrossFit — The "How's That Working Out For You" Frame

People walk through the door, find it interesting, find it hard, then go tell their friends or family they're thinking about CrossFit. Their friends who once did CrossFit and got hurt, or who heard from someone who did, will warn them: it's dangerous, it's hard, it's expensive, it's stupid, it's wrong.

The reality: before Javier did CrossFit, he used to get hurt. When he first started CrossFit, he used to get hurt. At 51, he rarely gets hurt. CrossFit does a good job of pointing out weaknesses and imbalances — and eventually you have to address those. Does CrossFit sometimes trigger chronic injuries that were already present? Yes. Does avoiding CrossFit solve those? No.

"The instability that's present under moderate or occasionally high intensity — it's present with no intensity. And eventually, intensity for you will not be a CrossFit workout. Intensity for you will be getting out of your chair, picking up your children, picking up your grandchildren, going to the grocery store, climbing stairs."

The person who has been avoiding pain for 20 years: their version of intense is getting out of bed. The person who's been doing CrossFit for 20 years: their version of intense is two back-to-back workouts.

On the people who left CrossFit after an injury and moved to a different sport: what doesn't change when you change sports is your ego. Most people don't leave their ego behind. They carry it into the next sport, and when they get comfortable, the injuries start all over again.

The frame OTL uses internally: "How's that working out for you?" Not as a taunt — as a genuine question. If someone is struggling with their weight, struggling with injury, doing nothing, listening to the person on the couch tell them not to try the thing that might actually help them — the honest question is: what is the alternative actually delivering?

What people need — and often can't get anywhere else — is accountability, motivation, guidance, a watchful coach, the encouragement of a community, and the pressure of showing up day after day. The people telling them not to try CrossFit are not providing any of those things for them or for themselves.

## Kids and the OS Baseline — How It Actually Installs

Source: voice memo 2026-02-17_1425_the-crossing-9

The profile of a kid who comes through OTL's kids or teens programs and stays through adulthood is almost always a kid whose parents are also at the gym.

Why: kids install their OS baseline — their default operating system for health and movement — from their families. For kids whose parents are at OTL while they're at OTL, fitness is what the family does. It's associated with mom, dad, the family default. When school sports end, nothing changes — the lifestyle continues.

For kids who are dropped off by parents who are not themselves committed to the program: those kids rarely stay into teens, and almost never become adult members. They use OTL as a drop-off program. The parents never witness what's happening in class. There's no family context reinforcing it. The installation doesn't take.

OTL programs kids and teens simultaneously with adults for this specific reason. The parent working out on one side of the gym while their kid works out on the other side is not a logistics decision — it is the mechanism by which the OS baseline gets installed. The kid sees it. The parent is present. The habit forms at the family level, not the individual level.

Javier's kids were homeschooled and CrossFit was treated like a school subject — not optional. They did math, they did CrossFit. They did English, they did CrossFit. It became part of their operating system. That's the model OTL is trying to replicate across member families.
